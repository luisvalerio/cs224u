Lesson 2 · Rules are cheaper than lists

Here's a small neural network that has never seen this sentence: the pandas escaped the zoo. Pandas: the bear, or the Python library? Nobody told it which. Yet it's almost certain: animal. Stranger still: the word pandas barely matters to it. By the end, you'll know exactly why.

What does a network actually read? Last time, a neuron asked two questions: is python in the sentence? Is pandas? Real language needs a question for every word in its dictionary: say 50,000. One thing we can do is put the sentence into a bag and shake it. The order is gone; only which words are inside remains. Mark those words in the dictionary, where every tick is one word, and that's a bag of words. A neuron reads the bag like a vote. For now, we set the votes by hand: snake votes strongly for animal, import votes against, and the word "the" barely votes. Words not in the bag stay quiet. If the votes clear the bar, it fires: animal.

So what can you build out of votes? Votes and a bar make a tiny programming language. Low bar: any animal word fires it. That's the OR gate. High bar: it needs python and pandas. That's the AND gate. A vote against gives you the NOT gate. Chain them, like last lesson's software-topic neuron, and you have OR, AND, NOT gates: what every computer is built from. So neurons can write any rule about which words appear. And one recipe always works: a list. One neuron per sentence you've seen, each saying yes to exactly that bag. Your turn: a new sentence arrives. What does the list say? Nothing. A list only knows what it has seen. And a row for every possible bag? Just writing down how many fills this wall.

Then how does it handle a sentence it's never seen? By storing a vote per word, not an answer per sentence. Watch the reach. A list row covers one sentence. Take a word rule: "zoo votes animal". It covers every sentence with zoo, even ones it has never seen. Rules are cheaper than lists. So who sets the votes? A network can do it from labeled examples. Let's look at one example it gets wrong during training: a snake hid in the woods. It should say animal, but it isn't sure. To fix it, the words in this sentence get the blame: each one's vote is nudged a little toward animal. Repeat thousands of times. Every nudge to zoo helps every sentence with zoo, so the votes harden into rules on their own. Animal words vote animal, software words vote software, and words that show up everywhere, like "the", barely vote. Your turn: pandas shows up in animal sentences and software sentences alike. What happens to its vote? It cancels out. Each side pulls it back, so the words around it decide.

Add two hidden neurons, and blame flows back to them too: that's backpropagation, the same method that trains nearly every neural network today. Here, one learns animal words, and the other becomes a software-topic neuron, like last lesson's. This time, nobody wired it. The last neuron just weighs their votes: animal votes yes, software votes no. Now: the pandas escaped the zoo. Zoo and escaped bring votes learned from other sentences, and pandas cancels out: animal.

Each word's two votes are its profile: how much it leans toward each concept. Tiger and owl never shared a sentence, yet their profiles match. Next, a closer look at word profiles: how they work in practice, and why they're far more powerful than the simple version we built here.
