Lesson 3 · Words that fit the same blanks end up alike

So far, a word was just a slot: one entry in a list of 50,000. The slot only marks which word it is: nothing in tiger's slot says it's an animal, like owl. So the field gave every word a profile: scores on a few traits, like how much it's about software, or about animals. Each bar is just a number. And those numbers are weights, like the votes from last lesson. In real models, nobody writes them, or even picks the traits. They start out random, and the network learns them by itself, from text. That learned row of numbers is called the word's embedding.

Here's a map of these embeddings, from a network nobody told what words mean. No labels, no definitions. It only ever played one game: guess the missing word. Yet tiger and owl ended up side by side, even if they never appeared in the same sentence. By the end, you'll know how a guessing game builds this map, and what the map buys you.

What is a word, to a network? Last time, every word cast two votes: one for the animal neuron, and one for the software neuron. Not just yes or no: each vote has a strength, called its weight. Those two weights are just two numbers, so we can plot every embedding on a map. Words that act alike sit close together: tiger next to owl, csv far across the map. That's an embedding: a profile of numbers for each word. It's just each word's votes for the first neurons. But lesson 2 drew this map from labels: we told it which sentences were about animals. Real text has no labels.

So without labels, where do the profiles come from? The field's big discovery: text can label itself. Hide a word, and have the network guess it from its neighbors. The hidden word is the answer, so any text becomes a lesson. The neighbors' profiles point to a spot on the map, and the guess is the word closest to it. On a wrong guess, backpropagation pulls the right word toward that spot, like the votes last time. Here's the trick. Tiger and owl never share a sentence. But they fill the same blanks.

The words start scattered at random. After thousands of rounds, owl ends up right next to tiger. Same blanks, same nudges, same place. Each word gets only two numbers, so it can't keep a private answer for every blank. Words that fill the same blanks have to share. A linguist put it this way in 1957: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” And notice: the map's sides have no names. Nobody named the traits; only the neighborhoods mean something.

So what do you get for it? Teach it a task with just two labels: tiger is an animal, csv is software. One neuron reads each word's profile, and one neuron makes one straight cut: a line across the map. Your turn: it was only taught tiger and csv. What does it say about owl? Animal. Owl sits on tiger's side of the line, and so does every animal near it. Last lesson, a rule was a vote per word. Now a rule is a cut through the map: teach one word, and its whole neighborhood learns. That's why language models first read mountains of raw text. The guessing game builds the map; then a few examples go a long way.

Our map has two numbers per word. Real models use hundreds, even thousands, because words differ in many ways at once. You can't draw that map, but closeness still works. In real profiles with 100 numbers, Tuesday and Wednesday line up almost perfectly. Tuesday's nearest neighbors are the other weekdays. Tiger's are tigers, leopard, elephant, and woods.

That woods is a clue. Each word gets one place, so a word with two meanings gets stuck in between. Our pandas fills zoo blanks and software blanks, and lands between the two neighborhoods. Real profiles do it too: python sits next to monty and cleese, and next to php and perl. The words around it still have to decide. Next: a neuron nobody asked for.
