Lesson 4 · To guess the next letter, it learned the mood

This network reads product reviews one letter at a time. It was trained to do one thing, and one thing only: guess the next letter. Now watch one of its neurons while it reads this customer review. It climbs as the praise piles up, and drops the moment the complaint begins. Nobody told it what happy means. It never saw a star rating. So why would a letter-guesser grow a mood detector?

What does the mood have to do with the next letter? First, the game. The network reads a review, and before each letter, it bets on what comes next. Its first bets are spread out. A few letters into the word “great”, it's already sure. Then it sees the real letter, gets nudged toward it, and moves on. Each review is its own answer key. That's the whole job. No stars, no labels, nothing about mood. In 2017, researchers at OpenAI played it over 82 million Amazon reviews: a month of guessing, one letter at a time.

Your turn: how does this review go on? Not strong enough. That's what the network writes. Change only the first sentence to: “Best thing I bought this year,” and the same words go on: “a great price.” The words right before the blank were the same. Only the mood was different, and the mood picked the next letters. It happens all through a review. After “I would”, a happy review bets on r, for recommend. An unhappy one bets on n, for not. After “Overall I am very”: h for happy, or d for disappointed. The mood is set early and pays off again and again. So every nudge that helped it keep track of the mood made its guesses better, and those nudges added up. Nobody asked for a mood detector. Guessing the next letter well rewarded one.

So where does it keep the mood? To guess well, it has to remember what it has read so far. So the researchers gave it a memory: 4,096 neurons, the size they chose. After every letter, it updates them all. That memory never grows, however long the review gets, so it can only keep what helps the next guess. After training, the researchers showed it reviews they knew were happy or unhappy, and searched its memory for a neuron that tracked the difference. One neuron did, almost by itself. Put a single bar on that neuron, like in lesson 1, and it sorts movie reviews into good and bad, right 92 times in 100. So let's call it the mood detection neuron. Researchers call it the sentiment neuron. Nobody wired it, and its training never used a single label. It grew on its own, from guessing letters.

“I really love this blender”: up, as you'd expect. Your turn: “I really wanted to love this blender.” Which way does it move? Down. It reads the whole phrase, not just the word. A bag of words would have counted “love” as a vote for happy.

Does the network really use it? Here's the test: keep every letter the same, and turn only this one neuron. After “I would”: turned to happy, it bets r. Turned to unhappy, it bets n. Let it write, and a whole review follows. Happy: “This blender is sturdy, well made and works exactly as advertised.” Unhappy: “This blender was defective and did not work properly.” Same network, same first words. One neuron, turned.

Four lessons, one idea: a network that has to describe a lot with a little is forced to find what matters. With labels, it found rules. Filling in blanks, it found word profiles. Asked only to guess the next letter, it found the mood. And this network had just 4,096 memory neurons. Scale networks up, with far more neurons and far more text, and they have room to invent far more: concepts of every kind, and even circuits: groups of neurons that work like little functions and chain steps of reasoning. We'll open those up in another series.
